## Surrender Charge Benchmarks

Surrender charges are the most important cash value metric in the first 10-15 years. They represent the penalty for early policy termination.

| Policy Year | Typical Surrender Charge (as % of cash value) |
|-------------|----------------------------------------------|
| 1-2 | 100-150% (most premiums consumed by COI + commissions) |
| 3-5 | 80-100% |
| 6-7 | 50-80% |
| 8-10 | 20-50% |
| 11-15 | 5-20% |
| 16+ | 0% (typically fully vested) |

**Key insight:** A well-designed policy (low-load, optimized PUA riders) reaches positive IRR (cumulative premiums < cash value) at year 10-15. A standard commission-based policy may not cross this threshold until year 15-20.

**Policy design variants:**
- **Standard commission policy:** Highest surrender charges (100-150% Y1). Agent commission 50-100% of first-year premium.
- **Low-load policy:** Lower surrender charges (50-80% Y1). Agent commission 0-15% of premium. Available through fee-only advisors (e.g., Fidelity, Vanguard, low-load carriers).
- **10-pay policy:** Paid-up in 10 years. Shorter surrender period (usually 10 years then fully vested).
- **Single-premium:** Highest MEC risk (must pass 7-pay test). Surrender charges usually 7-10 year declining schedule.

## Cost of Insurance (COI) Benchmarks

COI is the largest single cost of a permanent policy (~75% of total premiums over life). COI is age-dependent and varies significantly by carrier.

**Approximate annual COI per $1,000 of Net Amount at Risk (death benefit minus cash value):**

| Age | Preferred Non-Smoker | Standard Non-Smoker | Preferred Smoker |
|-----|---------------------|--------------------|-----------------|
| 35 | $0.80-$1.20 | $1.50-$2.00 | $2.50-$3.50 |
| 45 | $1.50-$2.50 | $2.50-$4.00 | $5.00-$8.00 |
| 55 | $3.50-$5.00 | $6.00-$9.00 | $12.00-$18.00 |
| 65 | $8.00-$12.00 | $14.00-$20.00 | $25.00-$40.00 |
| 75 | $20.00-$30.00 | $35.00-$50.00 | $60.00-$90.00 |

**COI increases exponentially after ~age 60.** The COI at age 85 can be 10-20x the COI at age 60. This is why universal life policies often fail in later years if not properly funded — the rising COI consumes the cash value.

**2017 CSO mortality table** is the current standard. Prior: 2001 CSO. The switch lowered COI for most ages (longer life expectancies). Check which table a policy uses — policies issued before 2020 may use 2001 CSO with higher COI.

## MEC 7-Pay Test Reference Values

The Modified Endowment Contract (MEC) test under IRC Section 7702A limits cumulative premiums in the first 7 policy years to prevent overfunding as an investment vehicle.

**Simplified rule-of-thumb (annual 7-pay limit as % of death benefit):**

| Issue Age | Male (approx) | Female (approx) |
|-----------|------|------|
| 25 | $7.50/$1,000 DB | $7.00/$1,000 DB |
| 35 | $11.00/$1,000 DB | $10.00/$1,000 DB |
| 45 | $16.00/$1,000 DB | $14.50/$1,000 DB |
| 55 | $23.00/$1,000 DB | $20.00/$1,000 DB |
| 65 | $33.00/$1,000 DB | $28.00/$1,000 DB |

**Example:** A 45-year-old male with $1M death benefit has ~$16,000/year 7-pay limit. At $70K/year premium, the policy would MEC by year 3.

**Key implications:**
- The 7-pay limit DECREASES as death benefit increases (more insurance => more premium allowed)
- To maximize cash value funding, carriers use **term blending** — combine $1M permanent with $4M term to maintain $5M total DB while minimizing COI on permanent portion
- MEC status is **permanent** — cannot be undone
- MEC consequences: loans are taxable LIFO (gains first) + 10% penalty if under 59.5

**Computing via formula:**
```
NSP = Net Single Premium for 7-pay paid-up policy
= sum over t=1..7 of (q_x+t-1 * v^t * DB + t_px * v^t * Terminal_Reserve)
where q_x = mortality rate at age x
v = 1/(1+i) where i = min(4%, Moody's corporate rate)
DB = death benefit
Terminal_Reserve approximates to DB at end of 7th year

7-pay limit per year = NSP / 7
```

## Capital Equivalent Value (CEV) Framework

The CEV framework converts a cash value policy's net IRR into a pre-tax-equivalent return, enabling apples-to-apples comparison with taxable investments.

### Standard CEV Formula

From StackedLife analysis (35-year-old, $24K/yr premium, $500K DB):

```
CEV = Policy_IRR / (1 - Tax_Rate - Mgmt_Fee - Term_Cost)

Where:
- Policy_IRR = net IRR of cash value (e.g., 4.6%)
- Tax_Rate = marginal ordinary income rate (e.g., 24% + NIIT = 27.8%)
- Mgmt_Fee = investment management fee (e.g., 1.5% for actively managed)
- Term_Cost = cost of equivalent term insurance (e.g., 0.25% of face)

Example: 4.6% / (1 - 0.24 - 0.015 - 0.003) = 4.6% / 0.742 = 6.2%
Adding term insurance: 4.6% / (1 - 0.24 - 0.015 - 0.003 - 0.0025) = 4.6% / 0.7395 = 6.22%
→ At 24% bracket: 4.6% policy IRR = 6.2% pre-tax equivalent needed
→ At 37% bracket: 4.6% / (1 - 0.37 - 0.015) = 4.6% / 0.615 = 7.48%
```

### When CEV Analysis Is Most Favorable to Cash Value

| Condition | CEV Multiplier | Why |
|-----------|---------------|-----|
| High tax bracket (37% + NIIT) | ~1.6x-1.8x | Tax deferral most valuable |
| Long holding period (20+ years) | ~1.4x-1.6x | COI and surrender charges amortized |
| Already maxed tax-advantaged accounts | ~1.3x-1.5x | No alternative tax shelter |
| Low dividend rate environment (<4%) | <1.0x | Policy IRR likely negative in early years |
| Short holding period (<10 years) | <1.0x | Surrender charges dominate |
| Low tax bracket (12% or lower) | ~0.8x-1.0x | Minimal tax deferral benefit |

### Key Insight

The CEV argument is strongest for **high-bracket, long-horizon, maxed-out savers** and weakest for **low-bracket, short-horizon, or under-saving clients.** Most critics (White Coat Investor, Bogleheads) assume a low-bracket or moderate-bracket client; most proponents (Pfau, Nash) assume a high-bracket client with a 30+ year horizon. Both are right for their assumed profile.

## Policy Loan Mechanics (Direct vs Non-Direct Recognition)

**Non-Direct Recognition (preferred for cash value strategies):**
- Carrier credits dividends on the FULL cash value, including the portion borrowed against
- Net effect: Policyholder earns dividend on borrowed money minus loan interest
- If loan rate = dividend rate: **zero-cost leverage** (the "wash loan")
- Carriers: MassMutual, Lafayette Life, OneAmerica, most IBC-friendly carriers
- Risk: Carrier can change recognition type or dividend rate

**Direct Recognition:**
- Carrier credits dividends only on the UNBORROWED cash value
- Borrowed portion earns a lower rate (usually the loan rate minus a spread)
- Net effect: Borrowing reduces cash value growth — must account for opportunity cost
- Carriers: Penn Mutual, Guardian, New York Life, Northwestern Mutual
- Some carriers (Penn Mutual) still outperform despite direct recognition due to superior overall policy design

**Loan interest rates (2025-2026):**
- Fixed loans: 5-8% (set at policy issue, never changes)
- Variable loans: 4-7% (floats with Moody's corporate bond index)
- Wash loan condition: variable loan rate = dividend rate on non-direct recognition policies

**Wash loan mechanics:**
```
$100,000 policy loan at 6% variable rate
Non-direct recognition, dividend rate = 6%

Interest cost per year: $6,000
Dividend earned on borrowed $100K: $6,000 (non-direct recognition)
Net cost: $0 (the "wash")

Alternative: Direct recognition
Interest cost: $6,000
Dividend on borrowed portion: $0 (not credited)
Net cost: $6,000/year
```

## PPLI (Private Placement Life Insurance) Key Data

PPLI is available only to **qualified purchasers** ($5M+ investable assets for individuals, $25M+ for entities).

**Typical cost structure:**
| Component | Annual Cost |
|-----------|-------------|
| COI (age 45-65) | 0.3-0.8% of death benefit |
| M&E (Mortality & Expense) | 0.25-0.50% |
| Admin fees | $500-$2,000 flat |
| Investment management (sub-account) | 0.30-1.50% (varies by strategy) |
| PPLI structuring fee (one-time) | $10K-$50K |
| Total annual drag | 0.8-2.5% vs 1.5-3.5% for taxable alternatives |

**Tax savings (Colva Services, 2026):**
- PPLI-wrapped hedge fund portfolio: +300 bp after-tax return over 10+ years
- Break-even: typically 7-12 years (structuring costs + COI recouped by tax savings)
- Best for: Alternative assets (hedge funds, PE, real estate, private credit) producing short-term gains and ordinary income

**CapRock UHNW recommendation (2026):**
- $5M-$10M minimum for PPLI to be economically viable
- Best use: tax-inefficient strategies (high-turnover, derivatives, credit, distressed)
- Alternatives: municipal bonds, direct indexing with TLH (simpler, cheaper for $1M-$5M)

## Common Research Pitfalls — Cash Value Life Insurance

1. **Dividend rates are NOT guaranteed.** The current rate (6.0-6.6%) is the highest in 15 years. Default analysis should show 3 scenarios: current, guaranteed (typically 2-3%), and midpoint (4-4.5%). Do NOT project current rates into the future without a stress scenario.

2. **Policy illustrations are notoriously optimistic.** Carrier-issued illustrations assume current dividend rates continue for 30+ years. The NAIC Model Regulation #582 requires policies to show both current and guaranteed rates — but illustration boilerplate is rarely read. Always use the guaranteed column as the floor.

3. **The White Coat Investor critique is valid for 80% of buyers.** WCI's argument: whole life underperforms "buy term and invest the difference" for most clients because: (a) early-year returns are negative (commissions + COI), (b) the surrender period locks in losses, (c) the client could have bought term and invested the premium difference in index funds. Counterargument (Pfau): for high-bracket clients with maxed-out tax-advantaged accounts, the policy's tax deferral + capital equivalent value creates net advantage at 20+ year horizons. Both are true for their assumed profile.

4. **MEC by $1 = permanent problem.** Accidentally exceeding the 7-pay limit by $1 permanently MECs the policy. The compliance checker should recommend staying 5% below the limit and requiring advisor confirmation before funding within 10% of the limit.

5. **Loan balances grow silently.** Policy loans at 5-8% interest, if not repaid, compound. Over 15-20 years, the loan balance can exceed the cash value, triggering a forced policy lapse with phantom income (taxable gain reported on 1099-R while receiving $0 cash). This is the most expensive and least understood risk.
