# PL 104-95 / 4 U.S.C. § 114 — Nonresident Retirement Income Tax Protection

## Statute Overview

**Public Law 104-95** (Pension Income Tax Limits Act) — signed January 10, 1996. Codified at **4 U.S.C. § 114**, also known as the "Federal Source Tax Act."

**Core rule (4 U.S.C. § 114(a)):** "No State may impose an income tax on any retirement income of an individual who is not a resident or domiciliary of such State (as determined under the laws of such State)."

**Retroactive application:** Covers amounts received after December 31, 1995.

**2006 amendment (Pub. L. 109-264):** Expanded coverage to include retired partner retirement payments under written plans effective before retirement began.

## Protected "Retirement Income" Categories (4 U.S.C. § 114(b)(1))

| Category | IRC Reference | Examples |
|---|---|---|
| (A) | IRC § 401(a) + § 501(a) | Traditional 401(k), 403(b) plans, qualified pension |
| (B) | IRC § 408(k) | SEP IRA distributions |
| (C) | IRC § 403(a) | Tax-sheltered annuity distributions |
| (D) | IRC § 403(b) | Same as (C) — cross-reference |
| (E) | IRC § 408(l) | SAR-SEP distributions |
| (F) | IRC § 408(a)/(b) | Traditional IRA distributions |
| (G) | IRC § 408A | Roth IRA qualified distributions |
| (H) | IRC § 501(c)(18) | Donor-advised fund trusts |
| (I) | IRC § 3121(v)(2)(C) | NQDC exceeding § 401(a)(17) or § 415 limits |

## Explicitly NOT Protected

| Income Type | Why | Planning Implication |
|---|---|---|
| Capital gains | Investment income, not retirement income | Still taxable by former state |
| Dividends | Investment income, not retirement income | Still taxable by former state |
| Interest income | Investment income, not retirement income | Still taxable by former state |
| Military retired pay | Explicit exception under § 114(b)(2) | States CAN tax military pensions |
| Social Security | Separately exempt by federal law, not PL 104-95 | Already exempt federally; state rules vary |
| Rental income | Not retirement income | Still taxable by former state |
| Business income | Not retirement income | Still taxable by former state |
| Non-periodic NQDC | May not qualify under § 3121(v)(2)(C) | Must be periodic/lifetime or 10+ year |

## Domicile vs. Residence — Critical Legal Distinction

The statute protects income of individuals who are "not a resident OR domiciliary" of the taxing state. **Domicile** is a legal concept requiring intent to abandon the old domicile and establish a new one.

**12 domicile factors (weighted for scoring):**
1. Voter registration in new state (10 pts)
2. Driver's license in new state (10 pts)
3. Primary residence in new state (15 pts)
4. >183 days spent in new state (10 pts)
5. Will/trust address in new state (10 pts)
6. Bank accounts in new state (5 pts)
7. Doctor/dentist in new state (5 pts)
8. Spouse/children in new state (10 pts)
9. Old state tax filing as nonresident (10 pts)
10. Property owned in new state (5 pts)
11. Property sold in old state (5 pts)
12. Mail forwarding stopped in old state (5 pts)

**Score <60:** Protection may not hold up in domicile audit. Recommend state tax attorney consultation.

## High-Risk Domicile Audit States

States that aggressively pursue domicile audits of wealthy former residents:
- **New York** — Has pursued 6-figure domicile audits against former residents
- **California** — Successfully asserts continued taxation of former residents' income
- **New Jersey** — Active domicile enforcement
- **Massachusetts** — Moderate audit activity
- **Pennsylvania** — Moderate audit activity

## Part-Year Resident Transition Year

During the year of relocation, both states may claim tax. Complex apportionment rules apply based on:
- Days spent in each state
- Income type (retirement income vs. investment income)
- State-specific apportionment formulas
